Slide 1

The Era Before PageRank (1990–1998)

How early search engines worked — and why they all failed at the same fundamental problem.

Problem 1

Keyword stuffing

Score = keyword count. Quality invisible to the engine.

Problem 2

No quality signal

Trusted gov page and spam page saying same words = identical score. Engines couldn't distinguish reputation from repetition.

Problem 3

Spam arms race

Engine penalises density → spammer hides text → engine detects → spammer cloaks → repeat. No stable equilibrium.

The fundamental flaw: All pre-PageRank engines judged pages only by what they said about themselves.
PageRank's breakthrough (1998): Ask what other pages say — a hyperlink A→B is an endorsement, like an academic citation.
Slides 2–3

Anchor Text & The Web as a Graph

The clickable text of a hyperlink often describes the target page better than the target page's own content — the IBM example.

Slide 17

Citation Analysis & Bibliometrics

Link analysis has intellectual roots in academic citation analysis. The same endorsement logic that measures journal authority applies directly to web pages.

Slide 4

PageRank — The Core Idea

A random surfer clicks links at random forever. Pages visited more often have higher PageRank. Score = steady-state visit probability π(v).

Slide 5

The Teleport Operation

Two cases: normal pages follow a link with prob (1−α) or teleport with prob α. Dangling nodes always teleport. This guarantees ergodicity.

Slide 6

Markov Chains & Transition Matrix

The web graph becomes a stochastic matrix P via 4 steps: handle dangling nodes, normalize rows, multiply by (1−α), add α/N.

Slide 7

Ergodicity & Steady-State Theorem

Two conditions — irreducibility and aperiodicity — both satisfied by teleportation. Together they guarantee a unique steady-state distribution π.

Slide 8

Computing PageRank — Power Iteration

Start from any distribution, repeatedly multiply by P until convergence. Typically 50–100 iterations for web-scale graphs.

Slide 9

Topic-Specific & Personalized PageRank

Restrict teleportation to a topic-specific set S. Personalized scores are linear combinations of pre-computed topic vectors — no per-user recomputation needed.

Slides 10–11

Hubs & Authorities — HITS

Good hubs link to many good authorities. Good authorities are linked by many good hubs. Mutually reinforcing — resolved by iterative eigenvector computation.

Slide 12

HITS — Choosing the Web Subset

Root set: ~200 text-matching pages. Base set: expand with in-neighbors (potential hubs) and out-neighbors (potential authorities).

Slide 13

PageRank vs. HITS — Comparison

PageRank is static and global; HITS is dynamic and query-specific. In practice, major search engines moved away from HITS while PageRank variants remain foundational.

Slide 14

Link Spam & Spam Detection

Link farms, anchor text spam, and Google bombing all exploit the quality signals used by PageRank and HITS. TrustRank propagates trust from known-good seed pages.

Slide 19

The Structure of the Web Graph

Broder et al. (2000) found the web has a "bow-tie" structure: SCC core (~25%), IN, OUT, and tendrils. In-degree follows a power law — most pages have few links, a few have enormous inlinks.
